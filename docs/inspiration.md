# Inspiration

This is a working reminder of ideas that have influenced Gatherbrain. It is not a full bibliography, and it is not a claim that the app implements any one method directly.

The useful question is: what should these ideas make possible in future versions?

## David Allen And GTD

Getting Things Done is a capture-and-clarify system for commitments. The key lesson for Gatherbrain is that open loops consume attention when they are held in the head. Fast capture matters because the tool should let the user externalize the thought and return to the work that produced it.

Future reminder: make capture cheap, make review possible, and help facts become next actions without forcing every fact to be processed at creation time.

## Tiago Forte And PARA

Tiago Forte's PARA model organizes material by actionability: projects, areas, resources, and archives. The lesson is not that Gatherbrain needs those exact buckets. The lesson is that organization should serve the user's current use.

Future reminder: contexts should be flexible enough to represent projects, people, meetings, areas, sources, questions, dates, or temporary workspaces.

## Time Block Planning

Time block planning is older than modern productivity writing. Its roots are in calendars, day books, appointment schedules, and daily plans; Benjamin Franklin is a common early example of someone blocking the day into planned activities.

Cal Newport did not invent time blocking, but he has popularized a rigorous modern version of it, especially as a support for deep work. The lesson for Gatherbrain is that attention can be planned as explicitly as tasks can be captured.

Future reminder: planner features should connect time to context, not just to calendar labels. `:now` should make the current plan operational.

## Vannevar Bush

Vannevar Bush's 1945 essay "As We May Think" described the Memex, a personal memory machine built around associative trails. The inspiring idea is not the imagined microfilm desk itself. It is the thought that a person should be able to build, follow, save, and share trails through a body of knowledge.

Future reminder: Gatherbrain should make relationships and trails easier to form over time, especially when facts start as small unprocessed captures.

## Douglas Engelbart

Douglas Engelbart's work on augmenting human intellect points toward tools that expand a person's ability to think, navigate, and act. The mouse and hypertext matter, but the deeper idea is augmentation: computers should help people handle complexity better together and alone.

Future reminder: do not treat Gatherbrain as a passive archive. Treat it as an augmentation surface for recall, planning, linking, editing, and decision support.

## Niklas Luhmann And Zettelkasten

The Zettelkasten tradition, especially Niklas Luhmann's practice, emphasizes small notes, durable addresses, and links between ideas. Gatherbrain's "fact" borrows from that spirit: a small unit that can be identified, related, moved, filtered, and reused.

Future reminder: preserve atomicity without making capture fussy. A fact should be small enough for software to route and rich enough for a person to recognize.

## John Sweller And Cognitive Load

John Sweller's cognitive load theory is a reminder that working memory is limited. Interfaces can spend attention through unnecessary choices, hidden state, and excess structure, or they can protect attention by reducing extraneous load.

Future reminder: command prompts, autocomplete, visible item numbers, default contexts, and simple storage formats are not small conveniences. They are part of the cognitive design.

## Unix And Eric S. Raymond

Unix is an inspiration because of its bias toward small composable tools, plain text, stable interfaces, and cooperation through files and streams.

Eric S. Raymond's *The Art of Unix Programming* is the book that made many of these design principles explicit for me. Gatherbrain borrows the attitude more than the implementation: keep the durable representation simple, let other tools participate, and avoid locking the user's memory inside a private database.

Future reminder: favor plain files, inspectable formats, scriptability, and small boundaries between parts of the system.

## Attention Is All You Need

The "Attention Is All You Need" paper introduced the Transformer architecture, but the inspiration here is metaphorical rather than architectural. Intelligence depends heavily on what can attend to what.

Gatherbrain has many attention-routing structures: current context, peek context, relations, lenses, due dates, timeboxes, and event trails. Each one changes which facts become salient.

Future reminder: improvements should often be framed as better attention routing rather than more storage.

## Be Where Your Feet Are

*Be Where Your Feet Are* is Scott O'Neil's book about presence, focus, and showing up where you actually are. For Gatherbrain, the phrase matters as much as the book: know the current context, the current focus, and the current commitment.

Future reminder: the app should help the user stay situated. Where am I? What am I looking at? What owns this time? What is the next useful move?
